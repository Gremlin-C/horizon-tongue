# Phonology — the sounds of the Echo

*The Horizon Tongue has two bodies: a **silent graph** (the canonical written form) and the **Echo** (the spoken form). Sound belongs only to the Echo — the graph has none. This page describes the Echo's sound system.*

One idea shapes every choice below: this is a language for a people who **forget** — who lose their memory on a schedule and keep what matters in a record. So a spoken word isn't really the *meaning*; it's a **handle** — a short spoken address that must reliably point at the right thing *even when the speaker's own memory is lossy*. Every sound is chosen to **survive a bad channel**, because here the channel is memory itself. The instinct throughout is **robustness over richness**: few sounds, maximally distinct, each hard to confuse with any other.

## Consonants (12)

```
            Labial   Coronal   Postalveolar   Dorsal
  Stop        p         t                        k
  Nasal       m         n                        ŋ
  Sibilant              s          ʃ
  Lateral               l
  Rhotic                r
  Glide       w                                  j
```

In IPA: **/p t k/, /m n ŋ/, /s ʃ/, /l r/, /w j/.**

- **Three places, nothing between** (labial, coronal, dorsal) — the three a mouth makes most distinctly. Fine in-between places blur first under load, and a handle that blurs points at the wrong thing.
- **One stop series — no voicing contrast.** /p t k/ are voiceless by default, voiced ([b d ɡ]) between vowels, but that's **automatic, never meaningful** (a p/b contrast is fragile and flips under noise). This is why a word like *nakawa* is said [naˈɡawa] — the *k* softens to [ɡ] between warm vowels, with no change of meaning.
- **The only fricatives are sibilants (/s ʃ/)** — the loudest, most survivable frication there is. The two also carry a faint sound-symbolism: /ʃ/ leans broad and warm, /s/ narrow and sharp.
- **A full nasal series /m n ŋ/** — nasals carry through noise, so they earn all three places.
- **/l/ and a deliberately loose /r/** — the rhotic's exact flavor (tap, trill, approximant) is never contrastive and never corrected; a learner's own rhotic lands the handle fine.
- **/w j/** — two maximally clear glides.

**The glottis is reserved.** No glottal sounds (/ʔ/, /h/) appear among content consonants — that whole dimension is set aside for marking *how a thing is held* (see **Stamp-particles**). Rule of thumb: **the mouth says *what*; the throat says *how it's held*.**

## Vowels (3 + 1)

Three content vowels — the corners of the vowel triangle, the most distinct system there is:

```
        Front      Back
  High    i          u
  Low          a
```

In IPA: **/i a u/.** No length, no tone, no nasal contrasts — all fragile distinctions the Echo refuses. Pitch and length exist as prosody, never as the difference between two words.

### The reserved center — [ə], the audible gap

The middle of the vowel space is **held clear**. The central vowel **[ə] is not a fourth vowel** — it's the one **un-landed** sound, with a precise meaning:

> A content vowel is a handle that **landed**. The schwa is a handle that is **reaching** — a thing known to exist but not (yet, or any longer) recovered.

For a forgetting people, reaching for a word and finding it gone is the *ordinary* condition, so the Echo gives it an honest sound. Where another language would fall silent or paper over the gap, the Echo says [ə]: *there is something here and I haven't landed it.* It's the tip-of-the-tongue made a real sound.

One hard rule follows: **a content vowel never reduces to [ə].** /i a u/ may soften under load but keep their corner — because letting a real vowel slide to center would be a landed word quietly becoming a gap. (Reduction you can hear is honest; reduction you can't is erasure.)

## The syllable — CV

Every syllable is **one consonant + one vowel**: obligatory onset, single vowel, **no coda, no clusters.** A word is a row of beats — CV·CV·CV — each a **grip** (consonant) and a **landing** (vowel): *lu·wi·na.*

- **No codas or clusters** — those are where a sound drops and no one hears it drop. If part of a beat is lost, the loss is total and audible (a missing beat), never silent.
- **Obligatory onset** — the consonant marks where each beat begins, so a half-heard word can still be cut into the right pieces.
- **/ŋ/ opens syllables** — with no codas, the velar nasal lives in the onset: [ŋa], [ŋi], [ŋu].
- **The gap is gripped: C[ə], never bare.** A schwa always has an onset. A *landed* beat is C + /i a u/; a *gap* beat is C + [ə] — the grip still says which direction you were reaching even when the landing is lost. A gap beat never bears stress.
- **Vowels never meet** — no hiatus, no diphthongs. What sounds like a glide is two gripped beats (/a.wa/, /a.ja/).

**Handle length:** a **content word is at least two beats** (if one blurs, its neighbor still pins the target); **one-beat words are reserved** for grammar/function particles. Two near-identical beats are disallowed: **/j/ never onsets /i/, /w/ never onsets /u/.**

## Stamp-particles (the how-held)

Nothing in the Horizon Tongue is said "naked." Every content word travels with a mark of **how it's held** — remembered, recorded, felt-now, or enacted — and these marks live on the **glottis**, kept clear of content so they can never be mistaken for it. A stamp is a one-beat glottal particle (glottal onset + vowel) that seats *outside* the content word.

The first built is the **performative — /ha/** (breath + open vowel): the live, outward, fully-committed act, *"I hereby…"*. It seats before the word it stamps:

> **ha luwina** — *"I hereby greet you: hello-forever."*

More stamp-particles (recorded, remembered, and others) are still being built — a sealed glottal catch (/ʔ/) for the *kept* family, an open breath (/h/) for the *enacted* family.
